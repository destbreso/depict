# The craft of drawn explanations

The rules here come from the people who make whiteboard explainers and from research on learning from pictures and words. The audit enforces the ones that can be measured; the rest are judgment. Where a rule is an inference rather than a finding, it says so.

## Plan the end before the first stroke

- **Know the final board first.** Andrew Park, the artist behind RSA Animate, learns a talk's beats by listening to it many times, sketches them numbered in sequence, and plans "the big flat end state" before drawing anything ([Rohde interview, 2025](https://sketchnotearmy.com/blog/2025/11/18/andrew-park)). In a board script that means writing `regions` and `links` before `beats`.
- **Choose the layout from the shape of the argument** (Mike Rohde's sketchnote layouts, via [Wilson](http://sketchyideas.co/sketchnote-layouts-the-ultimate-guide/); the mapping is an inference):

  | The argument | Layout |
  |---|---|
  | A sequence or a causal chain | `row` (or a `path` flow of regions) |
  | One concept with several facets | `grid`, the concept in the middle cell |
  | A comparison | two regions side by side, or a `grid` with one column each |
  | Layers or a stack | `column` |

- **Choose the picture from the question** ([Dan Roam](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-the-back-of-the-napkin/)): who or what is a portrait (an icon and a label), how much is a chart or a number, where is a map, when is a timeline, how is a flow of arrows, why is several things side by side.
- **Name the parts before the process** (pre-training: [Mayer & Pilegard 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning-segmenting-pretraining-and-modality-principles/DD24C2F48B9B1277CE59F78276110258), 13 of 16 tests, median d = 0.75). Draw and label the things first; then show what happens between them.

## Draw the thing itself

- **Show what the idea is about, drawn as it is.** A chip with its pins, memory sticks, a server rack, two names in one bucket, bars for a growth, a line and a curve for a gap. A picture of the real thing needs no translating back; an icon from a UI set reads as a button, not as the thing. Draw the focal thing of every region as a `sketch`, and keep icons for small secondary things.
- **Color carries meaning, not decoration.** Fill the shapes that matter (the row that was found, the gap between two curves, the new server) and leave the rest as line. The yellow of a highlighter marks what to look at; the accent is for the numbers and for emphasis.
- **How much is a chart, drawn to scale** ([Dan Roam](https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-the-back-of-the-napkin/)): compute the bars or the curve's points from the real numbers and say in the board's comment how.
- **Characters are the people in the idea.** A visitor, a user, you searching a list: drawn with a pose and a face that shows how they feel, and a bubble when what they say adds something. Not a stand-in for something that is not a person.

## Analogies, sparingly

- **An analogy has to map one to one, or it misleads.** Learners carry over the parts of a comparison that do not fit, and the misconception sticks ([Spiro, Feltovich, Coulson and Anderson 1989](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511529863.023)); an analogy helps when its structure maps cleanly onto the target's ([Gentner 1983](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog0702_3)). A forced one (a chef for a CPU, a library for memory) costs the viewer a translation and explains less than the real picture.
- **So explain the mechanism with the real things first.** Reach for an analogy only when the viewer already knows it well and it maps exactly; even then keep it as an aside, not as the board's structure.

## Composition: a numbered page

- **One layout that scales: numbered modules in reading order.** A textbook page, an infographic and a sketchnote's "modular" layout ([Rohde, The Sketchnote Handbook](https://rohdesign.com/handbook/)) number their sections and lay them out the way the page is read, left to right and down, so the reader follows 1, 2, 3 without a line to follow. Depict numbers every idea in a disc of the accent and lays the ideas out in balanced rows, as many rows as bring the board closest to 16:9: three ideas or ten, the closing picture is one poster with no empty corner, and nothing crosses the page to say "and then".
- **Proximity groups, distance separates** (the Gestalt laws of grouping, [Wertheimer 1923](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Wertheimer/Forms/forms.htm)): items of one idea sit close; ideas sit apart, a wide gap between modules; a link with `far` sets two things apart when their distance is the point.
- **One focal point per region, and visual weight in balance** ([Arnheim, Art and Visual Perception](https://www.ucpress.edu/book/9780520243835/art-and-visual-perception)): the thing a region is about is large, framed or marked; a region much larger or smaller than its neighbors unbalances the poster.
- **Things stand on a ground line.** Pictures in a row share the line their feet are on, the labels line up under them, and a figure casts a small shadow on the floor; nothing floats.

## The guide

- **An on-screen agent helps when it behaves like a person explaining.** Learners did better with an animated agent that gestured, looked at what it was talking about and showed expression than with one that stood still ([Mayer and DaPra 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027401), the embodiment principle); an image of a speaker alone does little. So the guide points at each new thing as it is drawn, its eyes follow the pen, and its face changes with the sentence; it stands aside, beside the idea, and never covers the drawing.
- **Cueing.** Its pointing and its surprise at a mark direct attention to what matters now (signaling: [Mayer and Fiorella 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/CD5B7AE1279A9AB81F8EEBB53DBEC86E)).
- **Animated by the principles of character animation** ([Thomas and Johnston, The Illusion of Life, 1981](https://archive.org/details/illusionoflifedi00thom)): squash and stretch when it lands, anticipation (a crouch) before it jumps, arcs in its hops, follow-through in its arms, secondary action (blinking, breathing), and appeal (a simple shape, big eyes, one color).
- **The same character on every board** is the house style: its cap takes the board's accent, so it belongs to each board and is still recognizably the same.
- **A pointing hand must not mislead.** Pointing directs attention, and a hand that has to cross one thing to reach another directs it to the first. So the guide points only along a clear line from its shoulder; when something drawn earlier is in the way it steps aside to get one, and when no place has one it only looks, and the pen, drawing the thing, is the cue.
- **What it is made of sets how it moves.** Squash and stretch and follow-through are applied in proportion to its material: a stiff body barely squashes and stops at once; a soft one squashes deep, stretches tall in the air, and its top keeps going after its feet stop and sways back, like a jelly (overlapping action, Thomas and Johnston). The same character always moves the same way.
- **A personality is a few habits, kept.** What a character does on its own, a favorite entrance, a quirk (the brain pushes its glasses up before it explains), is what makes it someone rather than a mascot; keep them few and keep them constant. A character made from someone's drawing keeps the drawing's proportions, colors and details: its maker should recognize it at once (references/characters.md).
- **Dressed for the subject, and never twice the same entrance.** A recurring character stays alive when it is not identical every time: a costume or a prop that belongs to the subject (a mortarboard, a hard hat, a ruler) tells the viewer where they are before a word is written, and a different way of arriving and leaving on each board keeps the opening and the goodbye from becoming wallpaper. One or two things, never one that hides its face.

## One sentence, one drawing

- **Each beat draws what its sentence names, while it is said** (temporal contiguity, 9 of 9 tests, median d = 1.22: [Mayer & Fiorella 2014](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/CD5B7AE1279A9AB81F8EEBB53DBEC86E)). Map images one to one onto the script, but do not illustrate every word.
- **Frames that worked in studies held one diagram, one or two sentences and two or three printed words, for 8 to 10 seconds** ([Mayer & Johnson 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.100.2.380)). At 140 words a minute that is one drawn element per clause, about 3 to 8 seconds each.
- **Labels are two or three words taken from the sentence, beside their object** (spatial contiguity, 22 of 22 tests, median d = 1.10). The audit warns at more than four words.
- **Nothing decorative.** Seductive details hurt learning (g = -0.33, [Sundararajan & Adesope 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4)). Every icon stands for something the sentence says.
- **About four new things before a pause.** Working memory holds three to five chunks ([Cowan 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/)); the audit warns when a beat draws more than four (an inference from that figure).

## Watching it drawn

- **Drawing as you explain works.** Drawings made live beat static ones (d = 0.54, [Fiorella et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000325)); tablet-drawing tutorials held viewers 1.5 to 2 times longer than slides ([Guo et al. 2014](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)).
- **Show the pen.** Watching an instructor draw helped when the hand was visible (d = 0.35) and not when strokes appeared with no hand ([Fiorella & Mayer 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000065)). The page shows a marker at the tip of every stroke.
- **Never push finished art in.** A drawing hand raised motivation over a hand that slides finished pictures in ([Krieglstein et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40561-023-00258-6)). Every mark on the board is drawn.
- **Secondary things go faster.** Practitioners pop or slide in what matters less ([VideoScribe](https://blog.videoscribe.co/the-ultimate-guide-to-videoscribe-animation-styles)); here notes and an arrow's label are written faster than labels, and a drawing that would outlast its sentence is a sign to simplify it.
- **Open with a picture, not slow writing** ([VideoScribe tips](https://blog.videoscribe.co/10-ways-to-make-a-winning-whiteboard-animation)): something a viewer understands within about five seconds. The audit warns when the first beat draws for more than seven.

## The look

- **Black line art, grey for shading, one accent for emphasis.** Park drew in black and red; more than one accent dilutes it ([TruScribe](https://truscribe.com/blog/whiteboard-video-hits-and-misses-making-the-right-choices-for-your-message/)). Marker kits give yellow to highlighting ([Neuland](https://www.neuland.com/en/products/visual-facilitation-kit)). A board has `accent` for the numbers, underlines and marks, grey for notes and shadows, a highlighter, and a few muted tints for colored-in shapes.
- **Weight and depth with marker techniques.** A grey drop shadow under frames, banners and arrows lifts them off the page; a banner colored in reads as the take-away; a wide filled arrow carries the main move. These are the graphic recorder's basic moves (Neuland's and Rohde's handbooks teach them early), done here by the kit.
- **Three levels of lettering**: a large title, region titles, labels and notes ([Carter, ISTE](https://cdn.iste.org/www-root/conference_med/excerpts/excerpts-4368.pdf)). Capitals only for a title or a short label; all-caps text loses its word shapes ([Staedtler](https://www.staedtler.com/us/en/discover/visualisation-techniques-for-flipchart-and-whiteboard-graphic-recording-sketchnoting/)).
- **Containers and arrows carry meaning.** Frames mark what matters (an item's `shape`); arrows set the reading order and say how things relate: solid leads to, dashed is weak or optional, double-headed is mutual (a diagram convention). Lines used erratically suggest connections you did not mean ([Wilson](https://sketchyideas.co/what-makes-a-good-sketchnote/)).
- **White space between regions, not inside them.** The gaps between the modules let the closing overview read as a map ([Staedtler](https://www.staedtler.com/us/en/discover/visualisation-techniques-for-flipchart-and-whiteboard-graphic-recording-sketchnoting/)); a region with three small things in a big frame reads as unfinished.

## The camera and the two modes

- **Canvas: stay near 1:1 and travel.** Lay regions out so the camera moves from one to the next, use more of the board rather than zooming hard, and end on a held pull-back to the whole board ([VideoScribe](https://help.videoscribe.co/knowledge/use-zoom-and-set-the-camera)). The kit frames each region as a shot, flies between shots along van Wijk and Nuij's path, and closes on the overview.
- **Leave earlier work on the board and point back to it** (RSA reuses drawn elements as a talk goes on: [Lines and Colors](https://linesandcolors.com/2010/08/27/rsa-animate-cognitive-media/)): a later beat can `mark` something drawn long before.
- **Slides: one idea per board**, built up stroke by stroke, finished with the complete drawing, then a fresh board ([Türkay 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2016.03.004)). The breaks between boards are natural pauses (Guo et al. 2014).
- **End with the take-away.** Prompting viewers to summarize helps them learn ([Mayer, Fiorella & Stull 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-020-09749-6)): close on a banner with the idea, a mark on the key part, or a live part to try.

## Words, voice and captions

- **Spoken to the viewer.** Conversational narration, "you" and "I", beats formal prose (personalization, 14 of 17 tests, median d = 0.79).
- **Captions carry the sentences; the board does not.** With a voice, sentences on screen as well as spoken hurt (redundancy, 16 of 16 tests, median d = 0.86); without one, the captions are the voice. A depict page writes each beat's sentence under the board, outside the drawing, as it is said, and the board holds labels; a video leaves the board clean and carries the sentences as subtitles beside it.
- **Captions for silent viewing**: most social video is watched with the sound off ([Digiday 2016](https://digiday.com/media/silent-world-facebook-video/); [Verizon Media 2019](https://www.streamingmedia.com/Articles/News/Online-Video-News/80-of-Video-Caption-Users-Arent-Hearing-Impaired-Finds-Verizon-131860.aspx)). Keep each to two lines of about 42 characters, read at no more than 20 characters a second ([Netflix](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977)); the audit holds a beat's sentence to that.
- **Length**: explainers run one to three minutes ([TruScribe](https://truscribe.com/blog/how-whiteboard-animation-videos-are-created/)), learning segments under six (Guo et al. 2014).

## Teaching a young child

- **Things, then pictures, then numbers.** Children meet an idea best through real objects, then a drawing of them, then the symbols (Bruner's enactive, iconic and symbolic modes, [Toward a Theory of Instruction, 1966](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674897014); taught in schools as the concrete-pictorial-abstract sequence). A child's board draws the blocks, the coins, the clock first, then the numbers they stand for.
- **One strategy, one step at a time, then try it together.** Show the strategy on one example, every step drawn; work a second example with the viewer; show the common mistake and fix it; check the answer. Never "and so on".
- **Their words, their numbers.** Everyday words; a new word is said, drawn and used again. Numbers and instruments as the child's grade uses them, never past them.
- **Slow and warm.** Half the pace, short sentences, a guide that is glad when they get it. Slow is right for a child and wrong for an expert: decide who the board is for first (SKILL.md, "Who it is for").

## Pitfalls

- A grid of boxes: every region the same, in rows, nothing larger than anything else. Compose a picture.
- A forced analogy, or a character standing in for something that is not a person.
- UI icons where the thing itself could be drawn.
- Too much text on the board, or whole sentences written out.
- Drawing too slowly: the viewer waits for the pen.
- Clutter: items crammed, arrows crossing, long lines across the board.
- Decorative icons that stand for nothing in the sentence.
- Music or sound effects under the explanation (they hurt learning: [Moreno & Mayer 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.92.1.117)).
